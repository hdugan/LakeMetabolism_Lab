Seasonal Metabolism

Lake Mendota in April, July, and October is practically three different lakes. The water temperature, how much sunlight gets in, and how the water moves (stratification and mixing) all change with the seasons — and so does the lake's metabolism. In this module you'll first predict what the seasons look like, then watch the lake's metabolic year unfold day by day, and finally work out why each season behaves the way it does.

A lake through the seasons

Lake Mendota is a typical north-temperate lake: it freezes over in winter, then goes through three very different open-water seasons. The physical changes in each one — light, temperature, and mixing — set the stage for everything that lives and breathes in the water.

🌱 Spring (reveal)

🌱 Spring

March – May

Ice melts, and the whole lake mixes.

As the ice goes out, the lake is cold and about the same temperature from top to bottom, so wind can stir the entire water column.

Days are lengthening and sunlight is climbing, and the surface slowly warms. Nutrients that built up near the bottom are mixed up to where algae can use them.

☀️ Summer (reveal)

☀️ Summer

June – August

Warm, bright, and layered.

The sun heats the surface water, which gets lighter than the cold water below. The lake stratifies into a warm, bright upper layer sitting on a cold, dark lower one, and wind can no longer mix the two.

Surface algae stay in the photic zone. The bottom water (hypolimnion) is cut off from the atmosphere, so its oxygen can run low.

🍂 Fall (reveal)

🍂 Fall

September – November

Cooling, darkening, and mixing again.

Days shorten and the sun sits lower, so far less light reaches the lake. The surface cools until the layers are about the same temperature, and the lake mixes top to bottom again — fall turnover.

Leaves, runoff, and dying summer algae add organic matter to the water just as the light fades.

Predict the seasons: light and temperature

Two measurements from the buoy capture much of that seasonal story: the sunlight arriving at the lake and the temperature of the water. Light at the surface vs. light under water

The buoy has two light sensors: one above the water measuring sunlight hitting the lake surface, and one 1 meter below it. Light weakens (attenuates) as it travels down through the water, so how deep the light reaches changes where algae can photosynthesize. Sometimes there are so many algae that they shade each other (self-shading), cutting off light for the algae below. The lake's metabolic year

Module 5 estimated GPP and ER from a single night. Now zoom all the way out: the same calculation, repeated for every day of a typical open-water season — March through November, averaged over 2021–2025 from the same Lake Mendota buoy. Slide through spring, summer, and fall and watch the lake's metabolism respond.

The estimates here use the bookkeeping method: the day-slope / night-slope idea from Module 5, with one upgrade — an estimated lake–atmosphere oxygen flux (from wind speed and how far the lake is from saturation, spread over the mixed layer's depth as measured by the buoy's thermistor chain) is subtracted from every hourly change before splitting day from night. It's the simplest way to estimate GPP and ER, which makes it a great way to learn the concepts. More sophisticated models exist — ones that fit gas exchange directly from the data and account for uncertainty day to day (see the R package LakeMetabolizer) — and they're what modern lake metabolism studies typically use. For the broad seasonal patterns below, though, bookkeeping tells the story well.

The missing season

This chart starts in mid-March and ends in late November — not because nothing happens in winter, but because the buoy isn't there to see it. Every year, it comes out of the water before ice-in and goes back in after ice-out, because lake ice would destroy the buoy. Winter (and under-ice) metabolism is real, and ecologically important, but measuring it requires different equipment entirely.

Why do the seasons look so different?

Photosynthesis needs light and algae; respiration needs warmth and organic matter to eat. Compare the light and temperature you sketched with the metabolism you just explored. They don't rise and fall together — and that mismatch is what flips the lake between autotrophic and heterotrophic.

Who is photosynthesizing?

The buoy carries two pigment sensors. Chlorophyll fluorescence tracks biomass from nearly any kind of algae. Phycocyanin is specific to cyanobacteria — "blue-green algae," the kind behind most harmful algal bloom advisories. Comparing them shows which algae are active in each season.

GPP/ER use LakeMetabolizer's metab.bookkeep() (Cole et al. 2000): hourly night/day DO changes with an estimated atmospheric flux subtracted first, using k600 from wind speed (Cole & Caraco 1998) converted to a temperature-specific gas-exchange rate via the Schmidt number. That flux correction is spread over the mixed-layer depth, which comes from the buoy's thermistor chain (EDI knb-lter-ntl.130): the depth where water density first exceeds the surface density by 0.1 kg/m³, taken as the full water column (12.8 m) when the lake is fully mixed. Where a year's chain has no data (2024 has none before late September), the average depth for that calendar day across other years is used. Mixed-layer depth is still an approximation, and turnover periods remain the hardest to estimate. Estimates are run once per day across the open-water seasons, 2021–2025 (averaged by calendar day) (EDI knb-lter-ntl.129) with each year smoothed by a 7-day rolling median before averaging, since a single sensor blip can throw off one day's number.
